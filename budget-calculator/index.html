How to make a budget in four steps

  1. Start with take-home pay. Use what lands in your account after income tax, CPP, EI and other deductions, not your salary.
  2. List every bill you must pay. Rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, getting around, phone, insurance and the minimum on each debt.
  3. Add what you choose to spend. Eating out, shopping, subscriptions and fun. Check a recent bank statement so the numbers are real.
  4. Pay yourself. Put a set amount toward savings or extra debt payments each payday, then live on what is left.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada has a guide to making a budget and its own Budget Planner if you want a longer worksheet.

The 50/30/20 rule

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple guide: about 50% of take-home pay to needs, 30% to wants and 20% to savings and extra debt payments. The calculator shows your split beside it. It is a rule of thumb, not a test you pass or fail. In many Canadian cities rent alone can take a third of take-home pay or more, so a needs share above 50% is common. If yours is high, the fix is usually in one or two big lines, not in cutting every small one.

A worked example

The calculator starts with $4,200 a month take-home pay. Needs add up to $2,850, wants to $490 and savings to $600. That is 68% needs, 12% wants and 14% savings, with $260 left over. Moving that $260 into savings would bring savings to about 20%, right on the guide, even though needs stay high because of rent.

Common questions

What is the 50/30/20 budget rule?

It splits your take-home pay three ways: about 50% for needs like rent, groceries and minimum debt payments, about 30% for wants like eating out and shopping, and about 20% for savings and extra debt payments. It is a starting point, not a law. Where rent is high, needs often take more than half.

Should I budget with my gross pay or my take-home pay?

Use take-home pay: the amount that lands in your bank account after income tax, CPP, EI and any workplace deductions. That is the money you can actually spend or save.

How do I budget if I am paid every two weeks?

Every two weeks means 26 pays a year, so most months have two paydays and about two months a year have three. Budget each month on two pays and treat the third pay as a bonus for savings or debt. Pick every two weeks in the calculator and it does this math for you.

How much of my pay should I save?

A common target is 20% of take-home pay, counting money to a TFSA, RRSP or FHSA, an emergency fund and extra debt payments. If 20% is not possible yet, any regular amount is a good start. Even $25 a payday builds the habit.

What counts as a need and what counts as a want?

A need is something you must pay to live and work: housing, utilities, groceries, getting to work, insurance and minimum debt payments. A want is something you choose: eating out, new clothes beyond the basics, streaming and hobbies. Some things are a bit of both. Put them where they feel honest to you.

What this calculator assumes

All amounts are monthly. Paycheques are turned into a monthly amount: twice a month is 24 pays a year, every two weeks is 26, every week is 52. It does not work out income tax, so start from your take-home pay. This page is information, not financial advice.
